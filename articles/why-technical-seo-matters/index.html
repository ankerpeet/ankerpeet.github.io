Why technical SEO matters when your website already looks good

A useful website still needs to be found. Technical SEO helps search engines understand what your pages offer, but it isn't a shortcut to the top of the results.

By Anker PeetOctober 9, 20265 min read

  3. What a technical scan can tell you

Imagine a business with a clear services page, useful answers, and an easy way to request a quote. Someone who reaches the page can understand what the business does. But if search engines can't find that page, or have trouble understanding it, some of the people who need those services may never see it.

That's where search engine optimization, or SEO, comes in. It covers more than technical checks: the usefulness of your content and how well it answers someone's question matter too. Technical SEO looks at the foundations that help search engines reach and interpret that content.

A good page needs a way to be found

Google describes SEO as helping search engines understand your content and helping people decide whether to visit your site. Its SEO Starter Guide is clear about something worth remembering: no change automatically puts a site first in the results.

Search has several steps. A crawler discovers a URL and fetches it. The search engine may then index the page, which means storing information about it for possible use in results. Ranking is a separate decision about which pages to show for a particular search. Being reachable doesn't guarantee indexing, and being indexed doesn't guarantee a high position.

For a business, that distinction matters. Fixing a technical obstacle gives useful content a better chance to be understood. It doesn't create demand for a service or make an unclear page useful. Both the technical details and the content deserve attention.

The details that help search engines

Many of the basics are ordinary publishing decisions, not tricks. A page title should describe the page rather than just say "Home." A service page should explain the service, who it's for, and what someone can do next. Links from other pages should make important content reachable.

For example, "Kitchen renovation services in Boise | Example Company" tells someone more than "Services." It only makes sense if the page really offers that service in that location, though. Adding phrases that don't describe the business isn't an improvement.

A few details are easy to misunderstand:

  • Descriptions: a meta description can give a useful summary, but Google may choose other page text for the search snippet. Writing one doesn't guarantee it will be displayed.
  • Crawl and indexing controls: robots.txt manages crawler access; it isn't a reliable way to keep a URL out of search results. A noindex directive controls indexing, and the crawler needs access to see it. Neither is a substitute for protecting private content with authentication.
  • Canonical URLs: these help signal which version of similar pages you prefer. They aren't access controls or a guarantee that a search engine will choose that version.
  • Intent: not every page belongs in search. A warning on a deliberately excluded page needs a different response from the same warning on your main service page.

The aim is to make the signals accurate and consistent, not to make every scanner warning disappear.

What a technical scan can tell you

My technical SEO review uses LibreCrawl to check agreed URLs and collect errors and warnings, such as a missing title tag. I review those findings before putting them into a report, so the recommendation reflects what the page is meant to do.

There are limits to that review. Each selected URL is checked separately, with the crawl restricted to one page. Sitemap discovery and following external links are disabled, and the scan respects robots.txt. It doesn't map the whole site's links or compare all your pages for duplicate content.

If a page is blocked or the scan fails, that is an incomplete check, not a clean result. And even a successful scan can't tell us whether your content answers the questions your customers ask. Keyword research, a wider crawl, and a content strategy are different pieces of work.

A practical place to start

Start with pages that have a clear purpose: your main services, important products, or an explanation people need before contacting you. A small, well-chosen review can be more useful than a long list of warnings nobody has time to investigate.

  1. Decide which pages should appear in search. Write down their purpose and the audience they serve. Keep account pages and private information out of that list.
  2. Check what the page says. Does the title match the content? Can someone tell what you offer without already knowing your business? Are important details available as readable text?
  3. Check the technical evidence. Investigate fetch failures and unintended indexing restrictions before polishing descriptions. Confirm the warning on the actual page.
  4. Use Search Console where available. Google's URL Inspection and indexing reports can help you understand what Google has seen. A local scanner cannot replace that view.
  5. Measure something useful. Search impressions and clicks help explain discovery, but relevant inquiries or completed tasks matter more than a score by itself.

Changes can take time to show up in search, and some won't produce a noticeable ranking change. Record what you changed and when, then give yourself enough time to look for a meaningful pattern rather than reacting to every daily movement.

Keep SEO part of the work

A redesign can change URLs, remove useful text, or accidentally carry a staging site's indexing restrictions into production. Those are good reasons to include a technical check when publishing, not just when traffic drops.

Accessibility, performance, and SEO also meet in the same pages. Clear headings help people navigate. Fast, usable pages help visitors finish what they came to do. None of that removes the need for useful content, but it makes the content easier to use.

In the end, SEO matters because people need a way to find the work you've put into your website. A technical review helps identify obstacles and gives your team a place to start, without pretending that search results are something a scanner can promise.
