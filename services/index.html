01 / Accessibility

Find out where people run into barriers.

I review the pages and user flows that matter most, using the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 at level AA as a reference. I also try the site with a keyboard, a screen reader, and browser zoom. Those checks help me see how the experience works beyond what an automated scan can tell us.

I'll explain what I found, who it affects, and how your team might fix it. I'd rather help make accessibility part of the normal work than leave it until the last minute before launch.

What I review

  • Keyboard use: tab order, keyboard traps, and whether menus, dialogs, and custom controls work without a mouse.
  • Focus: whether you can see it, whether sticky elements cover it, and where it moves after route changes and dialogs.
  • Page structure: headings, landmarks, lists, tables, and names for links, buttons, and other controls.
  • Forms: labels, instructions, errors, and whether people can recover when something goes wrong.
  • Color and contrast: text, icons, focus indicators, and information shown by color alone.
  • Assistive technology: screen reader checks and zoom/reflow at 400%.
  • WCAG 2.2 additions: target size, dragging alternatives, consistent help, and accessible authentication.

02 / Performance

Understand what's making the site feel slow.

I look at your Core Web Vitals (Largest Contentful Paint, Interaction to Next Paint, and Cumulative Layout Shift), using real-user data when it's available and testing the site myself.

Performance problems can take some digging to find. I'll trace them back to the likely causes and sort the fixes by what I think people will notice, so your team can focus on the work that matters.

What I review

  • Core Web Vitals: root causes behind Largest Contentful Paint: how long it takes for the biggest image or block of text on the screen to show up. Good is 2.5 seconds or less., Interaction to Next Paint: how quickly the page visibly responds when someone clicks, taps, or types. Good is 200 milliseconds or less., and Cumulative Layout Shift: how much the content jumps around while the page loads. Good is a score of 0.1 or less., with field data where available.
  • Rendering path: render-blocking resources, critical CSS, server response, and caching.
  • JavaScript: bundle size, long tasks, hydration cost, third-party scripts, and main-thread contention.
  • Images and media: formats, sizing, responsive sources, lazy loading, and LCP image priority.
  • Fonts: loading strategy, subsetting, fallback metrics, and layout shift from font swaps.
  • Network: request waterfalls, preconnects, compression, and CDN usage.

03 / Responsiveness

See how your site holds up on different screens.

A site can look fine at the screen sizes it was designed for and still be awkward to use on a split-screen laptop, a phone turned sideways, or a browser zoomed in for easier reading.

I try those sizes, including the in-between ones, and show you where the layout or controls stop working as expected.

What I review

  • Navigation: menus, sticky headers, and off-canvas patterns at every size.
  • Forms: input types, mobile keyboards, autofill, and error visibility on small screens.
  • Layouts: overflow, wrapping, grid and flex edge cases, and content reflow.
  • Touch targets: size, spacing, and gesture alternatives.
  • Content behavior: long words, translated strings, tables, media, and embedded content.
  • Orientation and zoom: landscape, text resizing, and browser zoom.

04 / SEO

Find what gets in the way of search.

A page can look good in a browser while giving search engines too little information about what it offers. I use LibreCrawl to check the pages we agree on for technical SEO issues, then review the findings before recommending changes.

The goal is to help search engines discover, understand, and present those pages. This doesn't replace useful content or guarantee a particular search position.

What the review includes

  • Selected-page scans: LibreCrawl fetches each agreed URL separately and reports technical SEO errors and warnings, such as a missing title tag.
  • Indexing intent: I review warnings in context rather than recommending that every page be made public or indexable.
  • Evidence and next steps: reviewed findings, affected URLs, priorities, and recommendations in your report and developer backlog.
  • Re-testing: available after changes to check the same pages again.

The scan respects robots.txt. It doesn't follow the site's links or discover sitemaps, so it isn't a whole-site link audit or a cross-page duplicate-content review. A blocked or failed scan is reported as incomplete, not a pass.

05 / Security

Review the protections around your pages.

I use ZAP's passive baseline to inspect the responses your site sends, looking for warnings such as missing browser security headers or cookie protections. I review the alerts for relevance and explain what your team could address.

This is a starting point, not a penetration test. It doesn't include active attacks or prove that your application, accounts, or infrastructure are secure.

What the baseline includes

  • Permission first: you must own the site or have authorization to scan it. We'll agree on the target and scope before starting.
  • Passive checks: ZAP observes HTTP responses for security alerts, including header and cookie configuration warnings where applicable.
  • Bounded reporting: the spider runs for one minute and may fetch linked pages; only alerts matching agreed URLs enter the report.
  • Reviewed findings: risk, confidence, evidence, and suggested mitigations, with priorities for your team.
  • Re-testing: available to check reported issues after fixes. A failed scan isn't evidence that an issue is resolved.

  • Specific recommendations
    Practical suggestions tied to the code and issue.

    We'll review the findings together, and you can ask questions.
